1. ～うちに

While the chance lasts · As a change develops

MeaningFormsExamplesComparisonsJLPT tips

Meaning in plain English

Picture a situation that continues for a while. うちに draws attention to what you do before it changes, or what changes as time passes. The two uses have different stories:

Use 1 · Take the opportunity
Do B while A is still true: eat while the food is hot, or visit somewhere while you are staying nearby. Waiting may make B harder or less useful.
Use 2 · A change develops
While A continues or repeats, B comes about: you become sleepy, improve, or notice a change. B is presented as a developing result. Practice can be intentional, and improvement can still be your goal.

Think of it this wayAsk: “What might change if I wait?” If that does not fit, ask: “What developed while this continued?”

How to form it

Noun + の
学生のうちに · while still a student
い-adjective
明るいうちに · while it is still light; keep い
な-adjective + な
元気なうちに · while still healthy; use な, not だ
Dictionary form
日本にいるうちに／練習するうちに · while in Japan / as you practice; the verb describes a continuing state or repeated activity
ている form
聞いているうちに · while listening
ない form
忘れないうちに · before I forget; literally, while I have not forgotten

Build it: 忘れる → 忘れない → 忘れないうちに. Use a plain form before うちに; put politeness at the end of the sentence.

Book examples, with clues

Read the Japanese, check the meaning, then use the clue to explain the choice.

Example 01

にいるうちににみたい。

I would like to try climbing Mount Fuji once while I am in Japan.

Clue Temporary stay + a wish to climb.

How to read this example

日本にいる gives the limited situation; 登ってみたい gives the wish. The speaker wants to use the stay as an opportunity before leaving Japan.

Example 02

はい、アイスクリーム。うちにくださいね。

Here is your ice cream. Please eat it quickly before it melts.

Clue The ice cream has not melted yet; eat before that changes.

How to read this example

溶けない describes the present “not melted” state. 早く and てください support the take-the-opportunity reading: “Please do it now, before it is too late.”

Example 03

うちにのをしてしまおう。

Let’s finish cleaning the garden while it is still light.

Clue Daylight is the opportunity; finishing the cleaning is the goal.

How to read this example

明るい stays in its い-adjective form. してしまおう means “let’s finish it,” so the intended order is: finish cleaning first, darkness later.

Example 04

をいるうちにしまった。

I fell asleep while listening to music.

Clue Listening continues; falling asleep happens during it.

How to read this example

This is the developing-result use, not advice to seize a chance. 眠ってしまった presents falling asleep as something that happened unintentionally here. The change can have a distinct endpoint.

Example 05

でも、をうちにようになりますよ。

Even with a slightly difficult piece, you will become able to play it as you continue practicing.

Clue Repeated practice leads to a change in ability.

How to read this example

重ねる describes repetition; 弾けるようになる means “become able to play.” The practice can be deliberate and improvement can be intended. うちに presents improvement as developing over that practice.

Example 06

がつかないうちにはなっていた。

Before I realized it, it had become dark outside.

Clue The change happened without the speaker noticing.

How to read this example

気がつかないうちに means “before I noticed / without my noticing.” 暗くなっていた describes the result the speaker found: it was already dark.

Do not confuse it with…

うちに ↔ 間に
Both may fit the same situation. 日本にいる間に locates something within your stay; 日本にいるうちに can highlight the chance you have before leaving. Review 間／間に.
ないうちに ↔ 前に
忘れないうちに means “before I forget,” with the feeling “while it is still fresh.” 忘れる前に states the order more directly. Notice the negative verb before うちに, but the dictionary form before 前に.
うちに ↔ たびに · Lesson 2
うちに can describe a result developing over an ongoing experience. たびに means “every time”: each occurrence of A brings B. Repetition alone does not decide the answer.
ようになる ↔ ようにする · Lesson 11
弾けるようになる reports a change in ability: “become able to play.” ようにする describes effort to make something happen or to maintain a habit.

JLPT approach

  1. Identify the use first. A request, wish, or suggestion can point to taking an opportunity. A change such as “became…” or “came to be able to…” can point to development.
  2. Read the negative carefully. 溶けないうちに means “before it melts,” not “after it stops melting.”
  3. Use clues together. Words such as “quickly” and “gradually” help, but do not automatically eliminate 間に. Check which emphasis the whole sentence needs.

These are reasoning aids, not guaranteed answer-key shortcuts.

Useful expression

今のうちに、やっておこう。

いまのうちに、やっておこう。

Let’s do it now while we have the chance.

Here, ておく adds preparation for later; おこう makes it a suggestion or personal resolution.

2. ～間／～間に

Throughout a period · Within a period

MeaningFormsExamplesComparisonsJLPT tips

Meaning in plain English

In A 間（に）B, A gives the time period. Look at how B relates to that period before deciding whether to add に.

間（あいだ） · Throughout
B continues, or is maintained as an activity or responsibility, over A’s period. You are describing what was going on throughout that time.
間に（あいだに） · Within
B happens, is accomplished, or develops inside A’s period. It does not have to last for the whole period. It can take time, happen more than once, or be a gradual change.

Think of it this way“Throughout that time” suggests 間. “Something happened or got done within that time” suggests 間に. This is a difference in focus, not a stopwatch rule.

How to form it

Noun + の
留守の間（に）／夏休みの間（に） · while away / during summer vacation
ている form
寝ている間（に） · while sleeping
Dictionary form: continuing state
日本にいる間（に） · while in Japan; いる already describes a state
ない form: continuing absence of an action
会わない間に · during the time we do not see each other; the context must establish a period
Adjectives
暖かい間（に）／静かな間（に） · while it is warm / quiet; keep い, and attach な after a な-adjective

Reading: Time-period 間 is read あいだ. Here, に changes how the later event is placed within that period.

Book examples, with clues

Read the Japanese, check the meaning, then use the clue to explain the choice.

Example 01

がをしている、たちはテレビをいた。

While their mother was taking a nap, the children were watching television.

Clue The TV watching is presented as continuing through the nap.

How to read this example

A is the mother’s nap; B is the children’s ongoing watching. Different subjects are fine. 間 emphasizes the activity over the period, not an isolated departure or completed task.

Example 02

わたしがでの、うちののをできないでしょうか。

Could I ask you to take care of my dog while I am away traveling?

Clue Dog care is a responsibility that lasts for the trip.

How to read this example

留守 is a noun, so it takes の. “Throughout” includes repeated care as needed; it does not mean feeding or walking the dog every second. The request politely asks for that ongoing responsibility.

Example 03

がをしている、たちはに。

While their mother was napping, the children went out to play.

Clue Leaving the house happens within the longer nap.

How to read this example

The mother is asleep when the children depart. 出かけた focuses on going out, not on playing for the whole nap. Adding に changes the focus from ongoing activity to an event within the period.

Example 04

わたしがでの、にがたくさんしまった。

While I was away traveling, a lot of grass grew in the garden.

Clue The grass growth occurs within the absence.

How to read this example

Growing is gradual, but 間に is still correct. The sentence reports the change that happened while the speaker was away. しまった adds an unwelcome-result feeling here; it is not a rule that selects 間に by itself.

Do not confuse it with…

間 ↔ ながら
For two actions done by the same person, 音楽を聞きながら勉強する means “study while listening to music”; studying is the main action. 間 can connect different subjects: mother naps while the children watch TV. The number of people is a clue, but the intended relationship still matters.
間に ↔ うちに
うちに can highlight a disappearing opportunity or development over time. 間に simply places an event or change within a period. A gradual result is possible with either; it does not automatically require うちに.
間に ↔ たびに · Lesson 2
間に asks “within which period?” たびに asks “each time what happens?” One long visit and something that happens on every visit are different relationships.
間 ↔ ついでに · Lesson 2
ついでに adds an extra task when doing a main task: “while I’m at it.” Sharing a time period alone does not express that convenient extra opportunity.

JLPT approach

  1. Read B before choosing. Watching TV is presented as ongoing; leaving the house is an event within that time. The same first clause can therefore take either form.
  2. Do not memorize “に = one instant.” Grass growing takes time. 間に still works because the growth occurred within the absence.
  3. Check the scope of “throughout.” Caring for a dog throughout a trip includes repeated feeding and walks; it does not mean one uninterrupted physical action.

These are reasoning aids, not guaranteed answer-key shortcuts.

Useful expression

留守の間、お願いします。

るすのあいだ、おねがいします。

Please take care of things while I’m away.

Use this when the listener already knows the responsibility. 間 covers the period of your absence.

3. ～てからでないと／～てからでなければ

First A must happen; until then, B is not possible or allowed

MeaningFormsExamplesComparisonsJLPT tips

Meaning in plain English

A てからでないと B sets a prerequisite: A needs to happen first. Until then, B cannot be done, is not allowed, or is impractical.

Read it from the result backwards: “I cannot decide” → “until I have consulted my family.” This is stronger than simply reporting two actions in order. A can also be a change you do not control, such as recovering from an illness.

てからでないと is common in conversation. てからでなければ expresses the same relationship with a somewhat more formal sound.

Think of it this wayLook for a required first step AND a blocked result. A negative ending by itself is not enough.

How to form it

First clause
Vて + からでないと／からでなければ · attach the full pattern to the verb’s て-form
する → して
相談してからでないと · not until I have consulted someone
取る → 取って
取ってからでなければ · not until I have obtained it
治る → 治って
治ってからでなければ · not until it has healed; an intentional action is not required
Second clause
決められない／してはいけない／無理だ／難しい · cannot decide / must not do / impractical or impossible / difficult
Need the て-form? Open the conjugation reminder
Group 1
う・つ・る → って；む・ぶ・ぬ → んで；く → いて；ぐ → いで；す → して
買う → 買って／読む → 読んで／書く → 書いて／話す → 話して. Exception: 行く → 行って.
Group 2
Drop る and add て: 食べる → 食べて／起きる → 起きて. Not every verb ending in る is Group 2: 取る → 取って.
Irregular verbs
する → して；来る（くる）→ 来て（きて）.
Where the negative goes
Keep the first verb in its て-form. 相談してからでないと already contains the negative condition; do not replace して with しない.

Book examples, with clues

Read the Japanese, check the meaning, then use the clue to explain the choice.

Example 01

「いかがですか。こちらのはすばらしいですよ。」「うーん。なので、とからでないとかどうかられませんね。」

Clerk: “How about it? This painting is wonderful.” Customer: “Hmm. It is expensive, so I cannot decide whether to buy it until I consult my family.”

Clue The decision is blocked until the family consultation happens.

How to read this example

Read the final 決められません first: “cannot decide.” Then find what must precede the decision: 家族と相談して. 買うかどうか means “whether to buy,” so the customer has not agreed to buy it.

Example 02

をからでなければをはいけない。

You must not drive a car until you have obtained a driver’s license.

Clue Obtaining the license must come before driving.

How to read this example

取る becomes 取って. The result てはいけない means “must not,” so this is a prohibition until the required first step is complete, not just a report of two actions in order.

Example 03

がからでなければはだ。

Strenuous exercise is impossible until the illness has healed.

Clue Recovery is the first condition; strenuous exercise is not feasible before it.

How to read this example

治る becomes 治って. Recovery is a change, not a deliberate action, which is perfectly possible here. 無理だ expresses the blocked result even though it does not end in ない.

Do not confuse it with…

てから ↔ てからでないと
相談してから決めます = “I will consult someone, then decide”: sequence. 相談してからでないと決められません = “I cannot decide until I consult someone”: a required first step.
ないと／なければ ↔ this pattern
早く起きなければ、間に合わない = “If I don’t get up early, I won’t make it in time.” This states a condition for success. The longer pattern specifically emphasizes waiting until a first step is completed. Clock times are context clues, not a grammatical ban on てから.
Permission and obligation · Lesson 10
してはいけない expresses a prohibition; しなくてもいい means “do not have to.” A negative verb inside なくてもいい does not make the overall meaning “cannot.” Compare written rules in Lesson 10.
A necessary condition ↔ a sufficient condition
This pattern says B is blocked before A. It does not promise that B will happen after A. Consulting your family makes a decision possible; it does not guarantee you will buy the painting.

JLPT approach

  1. Break up the long ending. 相談して｜から｜でないと → consult first | after that | unless that has happened.
  2. Check meaning, not just ない. 無理だ supplies a limiting result without ない. In conversation, the result may be left understood: 家族と相談してからでないと….
  3. Test the order. Ask “What must happen first, and why is the next step blocked until then?” A general cause, deadline, or warning may call for a simpler conditional instead.

These are reasoning aids, not guaranteed answer-key shortcuts.

Useful expression

確認してからでないと、答えられません。

かくにんしてからでないと、こたえられません。

I can’t answer until I’ve checked.

Useful when you need to verify something before answering. In casual speech, you may hear てからじゃないと or てからじゃなきゃ.

4. ～ところだ

About to start · In progress · Just finished

MeaningFormsExamplesComparisonsJLPT tips

Meaning in plain English

Think of ところ as a close-up of an action’s stage. The verb before it tells you whether the action has not started, is happening, or has just finished.

The ending can place that close-up in the past: 食べるところだった means “was about to eat,” not “had just eaten.” First identify the stage; then read the tense of だ／です／だった.

Think of it this wayLook immediately before ところ for the stage. Then look after it for the connection: statement, reason, arrival, or interruption.

How to form it

BEFORE食べるところ

About to eat

DURING食べているところ

In the middle of eating

AFTER食べたところ

Have just eaten

Dictionary form + ところだ
書くところだ／するところだ／来るところだ · about to write / do / come
ている form + ところだ
書いているところだ／しているところだ · in the middle of writing / doing
た form + ところだ
書いたところだ／したところだ／来たところだ · have just written / done / come. 来た is read きた.
Polite ending
食べるところです · use a plain verb before ところ; add です after it, not ます before it.

Conjugation reminder: For the た-form, use the same sound changes as the て-form, with て → た and で → だ: 書いて → 書いた／読んで → 読んだ.

Book examples, with clues

Read the Japanese, check the meaning, then use the clue to explain the choice.

Example 01

ロケットはところです。のです。

The rocket is about to lift off. It is a tense moment.

Clue 間もなく + dictionary form: liftoff has not happened yet.

How to read this example

飛び立つところ places the rocket just before departure. です makes the statement polite. The verb describes the stage; the English needs “about to lift off,” not “has lifted off.”

Example 02

、となりののをいるところをにされた。

During the exam, the teacher warned me while I was looking at the answer of the person next to me.

Clue ている gives the ongoing scene; を connects it to the teacher’s intervention.

How to read this example

The speaker is looking at a neighboring answer when the teacher warns them. 先生に identifies the person giving the warning in the passive 注意された. The に after 先生 and the を after ところ have different jobs.

Example 03

にしていたテレビドラマがところでが。

Just as the television drama I had been looking forward to began, the phone rang.

Clue The drama had just begun when the phone actually rang.

How to read this example

始まった gives the just-completed beginning; ところで places the ringing at that point. This reports a real sequence. It is neither the topic-changing “by the way” nor the hypothetical “even if” use.

Example 04

ケーキがところへたちがきた。

The children came home just as the cake was finished.

Clue The cake is just finished; the children arrive at that moment.

How to read this example

でき上がった is the た-form. ところへ presents the children’s return as coming into that scene. Here に could also locate their arrival at that moment; do not memorize a rigid distinction that makes only one particle possible.

Do not confuse it with…

ところだ／ところです
Ends a statement about the stage: “I am just about to…” / “I am right in the middle of…” / “I have just…”
ところだから
Gives that stage as a reason. 今、食べているところだから、少し待って。 = “I’m eating right now, so wait a little.” A request following it can support this reading.
ところに／ところへ
Someone or something arrives at that moment. へ highlights movement into the scene; に locates the arrival at that point. These often overlap.
ところを
Someone sees, catches, interrupts, or otherwise acts on a person in that situation. It is valid Japanese, not an incorrect replacement for だ. Read what the following verb does to the person or scene.
ところで: three different uses
Timing: something happened “at that point,” as in the drama example above. Concession: Vたところで can mean “even if,” often with an unhelpful outcome. Topic change: sentence-initial ところで、… means “by the way.” The spelling alone cannot decide.
たところ ↔ たばかり
たところ points to the moment just after completion. たばかり presents it as recent from the speaker’s viewpoint; that can cover a longer time. “Just” in English does not distinguish them.
るところ ↔ ようとする · Lesson 11
るところ highlights the stage just before starting. ようとする can express an attempt or being on the verge. If the sentence stresses “tried, but could not,” consider the attempt meaning.
Compare the two Vたところで readings

説明が終わったところで、質問しました。

When the explanation had just ended, I asked a question.

This reports actual timing: the explanation ended, then the question came.

今から急いだところで、間に合わない。

Even if we hurry now, we won’t make it in time.

This considers a hypothetical action and says it will not change the result. The た-form here does not mean the hurrying already happened.

JLPT approach

  1. Use timing words as supporting evidence. これから／間もなく often suggest “about to”; たった今 often supports “just finished.” 今 alone can fit different stages.
  2. Match the particle to the next clause. An arrival supports に／へ; someone catching or interrupting supports を; a reason can take だから. Avoid “this particle always means…” shortcuts.
  3. Check that a stage makes sense. This use needs a situation you can view as starting, continuing, or just completed. Do not attach it mechanically to every state verb.

These are reasoning aids, not guaranteed answer-key shortcuts.

Useful expression

お忙しいところ、すみません。

おいそがしいところ、すみません。

Sorry to interrupt you while you’re busy.

This polite expression acknowledges the listener’s situation. You can also hear お忙しいところを、すみません; を is often omitted here.
